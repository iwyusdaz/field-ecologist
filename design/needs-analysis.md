# Needs analysis

## Problem
Sampling, bias and uncertainty are core research-methods concepts that students
usually meet as abstractions (a formula, a textbook diagram). Two things are
hard to teach offline:

1. **The truth is invisible.** Real field work never reveals the true population,
   so learners never see how far their estimate was from reality, or why.
2. **Bias is invisible.** Telling students "convenience sampling is biased" has
   little force when nobody can compare the convenience estimate with the truth
   for the same meadow.

A simulation can do what a field trip cannot: generate a meadow whose true
population is known, let the learner sample it, reveal the truth, and re-run the
fieldwork 1,000 times to show the sampling distribution emerging.

## Audience
- Grade 9–12 biology / environmental science students (pre-lab or replacement
  for field work).
- First-year undergraduates in research methods or ecology labs.
- Instructors (teacher guide with a one-period lesson shape and a mapping to the
  school field trip).

Primary roles for the portfolio: K-12 biology/ecology curriculum, higher-ed
research methods and science instruction, education research, EdTech.

## Evidence base
- Virtual labs can outperform hands-on labs for conceptual science knowledge
  (meta-analysis, g ≈ 0.55; Putica & Stanišić 2026, Frontiers in Education —
  verified, see `research/sources.md` #6).
- The distinctive learning mechanism here is the **reveal + re-sampling**: error
  made visible, and the sampling distribution made concrete. This targets the
  documented misconception "more data always fixes everything" (persona Noor).
- Seductive-details research says immersive dressing must not compete with the
  task: hence the realism matrix — high detail only where the detail IS the cue
  (field marks, clumping), restraint everywhere else (no fog/insects/birds
  during tasks, wind minimized while counting).

## Opportunity cost of skipping this project
Without the simulation, instructors have static quadrat photos (no design
choices, no truth) or spreadsheets (no meadow). Both lose the spatial reasoning
— walking time, moisture zones, cluster visibility — that makes design choices
feel real.
